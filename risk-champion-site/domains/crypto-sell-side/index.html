Crypto Sell-Side

Digital Assets — Sell-Side Risk

Managing inventory, market-making, liquidity, surveillance and financial-crime risk at crypto exchanges, brokers and OTC desks.

Crypto sell-side risk management lives inside the intermediaries — centralised exchanges, market makers, OTC desks, brokers, liquidity providers and prime-brokerage-style venues — that provide liquidity and warehouse risk so that others can trade. Where the buy-side owns portfolio outcomes, the sell-side owns the plumbing: it quotes two-sided markets, holds transient inventory, extends leverage to clients, and must remain solvent and orderly through extreme volatility. Risk here is simultaneously about the firm's own P&L (inventory and market risk) and about the integrity and safety of the marketplace it operates.

The risk surface is distinctive. Market-making desks carry inventory risk (delta exposure from imbalanced flow), adverse-selection and toxic-flow risk (being picked off by better-informed traders), and hedging/basis risk across spot, perpetuals and options venues. Exchange operators additionally own the liquidation engine, insurance fund and auto-deleveraging (ADL) mechanics that must clear defaulted leveraged positions without socialising losses. Layered on top are market-integrity obligations — surveillance for wash trading, spoofing, pump-and-dump and insider activity — plus custody, hot/cold wallet operational risk, and the AML/CFT, sanctions and Travel Rule duties that fall heavily on regulated venues.

In Singapore, sell-side crypto intermediaries are squarely within scope of the Payment Services Act (DPT services) and the Financial Services and Markets Act (Digital Token Service Providers), with MAS setting demanding expectations on custody and segregation, technology and cyber resilience (TRM), AML/CFT (Notice PSN02 and the Travel Rule), business conduct, and — for stablecoin issuance — reserve and redemption standards. A strong crypto sell-side risk professional combines a market-maker's grasp of inventory and hedging with an exchange operator's understanding of liquidation and surveillance engines, all anchored in the MAS rulebook and global standards such as MiCA, FATF and IOSCO market-integrity principles.

Singapore

Key regulatory frameworks (MAS & statutes)

Payment Services Act (PSA) — DPT Service Providers (Exchanges, Brokers, OTC)

Monetary Authority of Singapore (MAS) · In force (PSA 2019; amendments phased in)

+

The PSA licenses entities dealing in, facilitating the exchange of, transferring and providing custody of digital payment tokens — the core sell-side activities of exchanges, brokers and OTC desks. MAS imposes prudential, custody, AML/CFT, technology and business-conduct requirements on licensees.

Key requirements

  • Hold the appropriate PSA licence (e.g. Major Payment Institution) with base capital and fit-and-proper key persons.
  • Segregate and safeguard customer DPTs on statutory trust, with robust hot/cold custody and key-management controls.
  • Maintain AML/CFT controls per MAS Notice PSN02 and comply with the FATF Travel Rule for transfers.
  • Observe business-conduct and consumer-access measures, including limits on retail incentives, credit and leverage.

Implications for risk

  • Custody, segregation and key-management sit alongside inventory/market risk as first-order sell-side risks.
  • Retail leverage and incentive restrictions constrain product design and margin offerings for Singapore users.
  • Financial-crime controls are integral to onboarding flow and market access, not a separate silo.

References

  • · MAS — Payment Services Act 2019
  • · MAS Notice PSN02 — AML/CFT for DPT service providers
  • · MAS — Business conduct and consumer access measures for DPT service providers (2023)

Financial Services and Markets Act (FSMA) — Digital Token Service Providers

Monetary Authority of Singapore (MAS) · Enacted 2022; DTSP provisions in force from 2025

+

The FSMA establishes an omnibus framework including licensing of Digital Token Service Providers, capturing Singapore-based entities that provide digital-token services (including to overseas clients) and aligning with FATF ML/TF standards.

Key requirements

  • Obtain a DTSP licence where in scope, including entities serving overseas clients from Singapore.
  • Meet AML/CFT, technology-risk, governance and conduct requirements under the FSMA.
  • Maintain risk management and controls proportionate to activity, with MAS able to impose additional measures.

Implications for risk

  • Cross-border sell-side operations must map licensing across group entities and jurisdictions.
  • Extends the regulated perimeter, raising governance and control expectations for intermediaries.

References

  • · MAS — Financial Services and Markets Act 2022
  • · MAS — Guidelines / consultation on Digital Token Service Providers under the FSMA

MAS Technology Risk Management Guidelines & Custody / Key-Management

Monetary Authority of Singapore (MAS) · In force

+

The TRM Guidelines set expectations for system resilience, cyber security, access and key management, and incident response — critical for exchanges running matching engines, custody wallets and liquidation systems that must not fail under load.

Key requirements

  • Implement strong access controls, cryptographic key management and hot/cold wallet segregation.
  • Ensure system availability, capacity and resilience for matching, settlement and liquidation engines.
  • Maintain cyber-defence, penetration testing, incident response and business-continuity plans.

Implications for risk

  • Operational and cyber failures can crystallise directly into client losses and market disruption.
  • Liquidation-engine and matching-engine resilience are risk-critical, not just IT concerns.

References

  • · MAS — Technology Risk Management Guidelines
  • · MAS Notice on Cyber Hygiene (as applicable)

MAS AML/CFT (Notice PSN02) & FATF Travel Rule

Monetary Authority of Singapore (MAS) · In force

+

MAS Notice PSN02 imposes customer due diligence, screening, monitoring and suspicious-transaction reporting on DPT service providers, and requires compliance with the Travel Rule for the transmission of originator/beneficiary information.

Key requirements

  • Perform risk-based CDD/EDD, sanctions screening and ongoing transaction monitoring.
  • Transmit and screen originator/beneficiary information for qualifying DPT transfers (Travel Rule).
  • File suspicious transaction reports and maintain records and independent audit of the AML programme.

Implications for risk

  • Financial-crime and sanctions risk are core to a sell-side venue's risk register and market-access controls.
  • Wallet screening and transaction monitoring feed both AML and trade-surveillance functions.

References

  • · MAS Notice PSN02 — AML/CFT for DPT service providers
  • · FATF — Travel Rule guidance for VASPs

MAS Stablecoin Framework & Market-Integrity Expectations

Monetary Authority of Singapore (MAS) · Stablecoin framework finalised 2023; market-conduct expectations ongoing

+

For venues that issue or list stablecoins, MAS's single-currency stablecoin framework sets reserve, capital and redemption standards, while MAS's broader supervisory stance expects fair, orderly and transparent markets and robust conduct controls from intermediaries.

Key requirements

  • For SCS issuers: hold high-quality, segregated reserves at least at par and honour redemption at par.
  • Provide fair and transparent trading, listing and fee practices and manage conflicts of interest.
  • Maintain surveillance and controls to detect and deter market abuse on the venue.

Implications for risk

  • Stablecoin reserve quality and de-peg risk affect both listed products and the venue's own treasury.
  • Market-integrity failures are supervisory and reputational risks, motivating surveillance investment.

References

  • · MAS — Stablecoin regulatory framework (2023)
  • · MAS — Guidelines on fair dealing and business conduct (as applicable)

World

Global frameworks market participants abide by

EU Markets in Crypto-Assets Regulation (MiCA) — CASP & Market Abuse

European Union / ESMA / EBA · In force (phased 2024-2025)

+

MiCA authorises Crypto-Asset Service Providers (exchanges, brokers, custodians) with governance, custody, conflicts and prudential requirements, and extends market-abuse prohibitions (insider dealing, manipulation) to in-scope crypto-assets — directly relevant to sell-side surveillance duties.

Key requirements

  • CASP authorisation with organisational, custody and conflict-of-interest controls.
  • Detect and report market abuse; maintain orderly trading and transparent operations.
  • Segregate client assets and meet prudential/own-funds requirements.

Implications for risk

  • Sets explicit market-abuse surveillance obligations that inform global venue standards.
  • Custody and conflicts rules shape how a venue separates proprietary and client activity.

References

  • · EU — Regulation (EU) 2023/1114 (MiCA)
  • · ESMA — MiCA market-abuse and CASP technical standards

FATF Recommendations & Travel Rule (VASPs)

Financial Action Task Force (FATF) · In force (Recommendation 15; Travel Rule guidance updated)

+

FATF requires VASPs to be licensed/registered, perform CDD, and transmit originator/beneficiary information with transfers — the global AML/CFT baseline for exchanges and OTC desks localised by MAS and other regulators.

Key requirements

  • Register/license as a VASP and apply a risk-based AML/CFT programme.
  • Comply with the Travel Rule and sanctions screening for counterparties and wallets.
  • Report suspicious activity and mitigate ML/TF risk of new tokens and channels.

Implications for risk

  • Financial-crime typologies (mixers, sanctioned addresses) directly affect market-access decisions.
  • Cross-VASP Travel Rule interoperability constrains supported counterparties and corridors.

References

  • · FATF — Updated Guidance for a Risk-Based Approach to Virtual Assets and VASPs
  • · FATF — Recommendation 15

IOSCO Principles for Crypto & Digital Asset Markets / Market Integrity

International Organization of Securities Commissions (IOSCO) · In force (recommendations issued 2023)

+

IOSCO's policy recommendations for crypto and digital-asset markets address conflicts of interest from vertical integration, market manipulation and fraud, custody and client-asset protection, and cross-border cooperation — a global template for sell-side conduct.

Key requirements

  • Manage conflicts arising from combining exchange, broker, custody and proprietary-trading functions.
  • Detect, prevent and address market manipulation and fraud through surveillance.
  • Protect client assets and provide clear disclosures and orderly markets.

Implications for risk

  • Directly informs surveillance-system scope and the separation of proprietary and client activity.
  • Vertical-integration conflicts are a recognised supervisory focus for large venues.

References

  • · IOSCO — Policy Recommendations for Crypto and Digital Asset Markets (2023)

US Regulatory & Enforcement Landscape (SEC / CFTC / BSA-FinCEN)

US SEC / CFTC / FinCEN · Evolving (ongoing rulemaking and enforcement)

+

US oversight combines securities and commodities law, active enforcement against exchanges and market manipulation, and Bank Secrecy Act / FinCEN money-transmitter and AML obligations — a major driver of sell-side compliance and surveillance standards globally.

Key requirements

  • Assess token securities/commodity status and register or restrict activities accordingly.
  • Meet BSA/FinCEN money-services-business registration, AML and reporting obligations where in scope.
  • Comply with CFTC anti-manipulation authority over commodity spot and derivatives markets.

Implications for risk

  • Enforcement risk affects listing/delisting, market access and product availability.
  • US AML expectations raise the surveillance and screening bar for global venues.

References

  • · US SEC / CFTC — enforcement actions and guidance on crypto exchanges and market abuse
  • · US FinCEN — BSA / money-transmitter guidance for virtual-asset businesses

Market-Maker Inventory & Delta Risk Management

Market Risk / Trading
Open demo →

What it is. The framework a market-making desk uses to manage transient inventory (net long/short exposure) accumulated from two-sided quoting, keeping delta within limits while capturing spread.

When used. Continuous intraday quoting and hedging, inventory-limit monitoring, and skewing quotes to mean-revert inventory.

Methodology. Track real-time net inventory and delta per token; set soft/hard inventory limits; skew bid/ask to attract offsetting flow; and hedge residual delta via spot, perpetuals or options. Monitor P&L attribution across spread capture, inventory mark-to-market and hedging cost.

Inputs / outputs. Inputs: real-time positions, quotes, fills, order-book depth, hedge instrument prices. Outputs: net delta, inventory-limit utilisation, hedge ratios, and P&L attribution.

Interview talking points

  • Explain inventory skewing — widening/tightening quotes to pull inventory back to neutral.
  • Distinguish spread capture from directional inventory P&L in attribution.
  • Discuss how inventory limits protect the desk during one-sided (toxic) flow.

Skills: Market microstructure · Real-time risk / Python · Hedging and greeks

Adverse Selection & Toxic-Flow Analytics

Market Microstructure / Risk
Open demo →

What it is. Analytics to detect when a market maker is being picked off by better-informed counterparties, measured through post-trade markouts and flow toxicity metrics.

When used. Client tiering, quote-width calibration, and deciding when to widen or pull quotes for toxic counterparties.

Methodology. Compute markouts (price move after a fill at horizons like 1s/10s/60s) to see whether fills are systematically adverse; estimate toxicity (e.g. VPIN-style order-flow imbalance); and adjust spreads or client limits accordingly.

Inputs / outputs. Inputs: fills with timestamps, subsequent mid-prices, counterparty tags, order-flow imbalance. Outputs: markout curves, per-client toxicity scores, and recommended quote adjustments.

Interview talking points

  • Explain markout analysis and what a negative markout says about flow quality.
  • Discuss client tiering and dynamic spreads as adverse-selection defences.

Skills: Microstructure · Statistics · Data engineering

Liquidation Engine, Insurance Fund & Auto-Deleveraging

ADLExchange Risk / Derivatives
Open demo →

What it is. The exchange mechanisms that close out under-margined leveraged positions, absorb residual losses via an insurance fund, and — as a last resort — auto-deleverage profitable counterparties to keep the venue solvent.

When used. Designing and calibrating margin/liquidation parameters, sizing the insurance fund, and stress-testing ADL likelihood in volatile markets.

Methodology. Set maintenance-margin and liquidation thresholds; model liquidation execution and slippage into the order book; size the insurance fund to cover bankruptcy losses to a confidence level; and simulate when ADL would trigger under gap moves.

Inputs / outputs. Inputs: open positions, leverage, margin tiers, order-book depth, insurance-fund balance. Outputs: expected liquidation losses, insurance-fund coverage, and ADL trigger probability under stress.

Interview talking points

  • Explain the liquidation waterfall: margin, insurance fund, then ADL/socialised loss.
  • Discuss why deep books and tiered margin reduce liquidation slippage and cascades.
  • Describe insurance-fund sizing as a tail-risk (ES-style) exercise.

Skills: Derivatives risk · Simulation / stress testing · Order-book modelling

Trade Surveillance & Market-Abuse Detection

Market Integrity / Compliance Risk
Open demo →

What it is. Systems and analytics that detect manipulative or abusive trading on a venue — wash trading, spoofing/layering, pump-and-dump, ramping and insider activity — to meet market-integrity obligations.

When used. Continuous market monitoring, alert triage and investigation, listing/delisting decisions, and regulatory reporting.

Methodology. Ingest order and trade data, build behavioural detection models (self-trading/wash patterns, order-to-trade ratios and rapid cancellations for spoofing, coordinated volume/price spikes for pump-and-dump), score alerts, and route to case management with on-chain enrichment.

Inputs / outputs. Inputs: full order lifecycle, trades, account linkages, on-chain data. Outputs: prioritised alerts, case files, suspicious-activity reports and typology metrics.

Interview talking points

  • Define wash trading and how self-matched or circular trades are detected.
  • Explain spoofing/layering signatures via order-to-trade ratios and cancellations.
  • Discuss balancing false positives against detection coverage.

Skills: Surveillance analytics · Pattern detection / stats · Regulatory knowledge

Hot/Cold Wallet Custody & Treasury Operational Risk

Operational / Custody Risk
Open demo →

What it is. The framework governing how a venue holds client and firm assets across hot (online) and cold (offline) wallets, key management, and settlement to minimise theft, loss and operational failure.

When used. Custody architecture design, hot-wallet limit setting, withdrawal controls, and operational-risk stress testing.

Methodology. Define the share of assets kept hot vs. cold, multi-sig/MPC key policies, withdrawal approval workflows and limits, and reconciliation cadence; model loss scenarios (hot-wallet compromise, key loss, insider) and required insurance/reserves.

Inputs / outputs. Inputs: asset balances, hot/cold split, key-management policy, withdrawal flows. Outputs: hot-wallet exposure limits, reconciliation breaks, and operational-loss scenario impact.

Interview talking points

  • Explain the trade-off between hot-wallet liquidity for withdrawals and theft exposure.
  • Discuss MPC/multi-sig and why single-key custody is unacceptable.
  • Cover proof-of-reserves and segregation to reassure clients and regulators.

Skills: Custody / key management · Operational risk · Controls design

Middle office

The middle office: flow, responsibilities & skills

At a crypto exchange, OTC desk or market maker, the middle office is the 24/7 control layer between the trading/market-making and treasury functions and wallet operations, settlement and compliance. It captures trades and inventory, reconciles the internal ledger against on-chain hot and cold wallets, marks inventory and produces P&L, monitors counterparty credit and settlement risk on OTC flow, and enforces wallet limits and withdrawal controls. It is the function that keeps client and house assets segregated, provable and within risk limits — the discipline whose absence defined the 2022 blow-ups.

Core responsibilities

  • Capture trades and maintain accurate real-time inventory across all venues and tokens.
  • Reconcile the internal ledger to on-chain hot/cold wallets and prove client-vs-house segregation.
  • Mark inventory and produce spread, funding and basis P&L with fee reconciliation.
  • Manage OTC counterparty credit lines, pre-funding and DvP settlement risk.
  • Enforce hot-wallet limits, withdrawal velocity controls and cold-storage sweep policy.
  • Support market surveillance, Travel Rule and AML/CFT reporting obligations.
  • Act as the independent control bridge between trading/treasury, wallet ops and compliance.

Workflow

What a day looks like

Typical roles: Market-Making Desk Risk Manager · Exchange Risk Analyst (liquidation / margin) · Trade Surveillance Analyst · Treasury & Custody Operational Risk Officer · Chief Risk Officer at a crypto exchange or trading firm

Morning

  • Review overnight inventory, delta and P&L attribution across market-making books (24/7 markets).
  • Check margin health, liquidation activity and insurance-fund balance from the overnight session.
  • Triage overnight surveillance alerts and prioritise potential wash-trading or spoofing cases.
  • Reconcile hot/cold wallet balances and confirm withdrawal-queue and treasury positions.

Midday

  • Calibrate quote widths and inventory limits with the trading desk based on flow and toxicity metrics.
  • Review margin-tier and liquidation-parameter proposals for listed perpetuals and new tokens.
  • Investigate surveillance cases with on-chain enrichment and prepare any suspicious-activity reports.
  • Assess listing/delisting risk for tokens (liquidity, manipulation history, regulatory status).

Evening

  • Produce the daily risk pack — inventory/delta, liquidation and insurance-fund metrics, surveillance summary.
  • Stress-test insurance-fund adequacy and ADL likelihood after significant volatility.
  • Update custody and operational-risk logs, reconciliation breaks and remediation.
  • Coordinate with compliance on AML/Travel Rule, market-integrity and MAS reporting.

Crisis mode

  • Monitor liquidation engine and insurance fund in real time during a volatility spike or gap move.
  • Manage inventory and hedging as spreads blow out and flow becomes one-sided/toxic.
  • Assess ADL triggers and communicate transparently with affected clients.
  • Handle custody/security incidents (hot-wallet compromise) and brief the CRO and MAS as required.

Stakeholders: Market-making and trading desks (front office) · Exchange operations, matching and liquidation engineering · Compliance, surveillance and AML/sanctions teams · Treasury, custody and security teams · Institutional clients, liquidity partners and OTC counterparties · Auditors, insurers and MAS / other regulators

Market-Maker Inventory Risk & Quote-Skew Simulator

“I built the real-time inventory and delta risk monitor a market-making desk runs — with quote-skewing, hedging and P&L attribution across spread, inventory and hedge cost.”

A market-making desk quotes two-sided markets and accumulates inventory. Risk must keep delta within limits, skew quotes to mean-revert inventory, and separate genuine spread capture from directional risk.

Approach

  1. Simulate two-sided quoting against a flow model, accumulating net inventory per token.
  2. Track real-time delta and inventory-limit utilisation with soft/hard limits.
  3. Implement quote-skewing that widens/tightens sides to pull inventory toward neutral.
  4. Hedge residual delta via perpetuals and compute hedging cost and basis risk.
  5. Attribute P&L across spread capture, inventory mark-to-market and hedging, and add a fat-tailed inventory VaR.

Stack: Python · numpy / pandas · Real-time / streaming sim · Dashboard

Talk track: The desk looked profitable, but my attribution showed a third of P&L was directional inventory luck, not spread capture. Turning on quote-skewing pulled average inventory 40% closer to neutral and cut inventory VaR meaningfully — so the P&L became more spread-driven and repeatable, which is exactly what a market maker wants.

  • Net delta and inventory-limit utilisation
  • P&L attribution: spread vs. inventory vs. hedge
  • Inventory VaR (fat-tailed)
  • Average inventory distance from neutral

Wash-Trading & Spoofing Surveillance Engine

“I built a market-abuse surveillance engine that detects wash trading and spoofing from raw order/trade data — the market-integrity control every regulated venue needs.”

An exchange must detect and evidence manipulative trading — self-matched wash trades inflating volume, and spoofing/layering that fakes order-book pressure — to meet MiCA/IOSCO/MAS market-integrity expectations.

Approach

  1. Reconstruct the order lifecycle (submits, cancels, fills) and link related accounts.
  2. Detect wash trading via self-matched and circular-trade patterns between linked accounts.
  3. Detect spoofing/layering via high order-to-trade ratios, large orders placed then rapidly cancelled, and price impact around cancellations.
  4. Score and prioritise alerts, tuning thresholds to balance false positives against coverage.
  5. Produce case files with evidence timelines suitable for regulatory reporting.

Stack: Python · pandas · Pattern-detection logic · Case dashboard

Talk track: My engine flagged a cluster of accounts trading against each other to inflate volume on a thin token, and separately a trader placing large bids then cancelling within milliseconds to lift the price before selling. I tuned thresholds on order-to-trade ratio and markout so the alert queue stayed manageable, and each case shipped with a timeline an investigator or MAS could act on.

  • Wash-trade volume detected vs. reported volume
  • Order-to-trade ratio and cancellation-speed outliers
  • Alert precision / false-positive rate
  • Cases escalated to reporting

Hot/Cold Wallet Segregation & Proof-of-Reserves Attestation

“I built the proof-of-reserves pipeline — reconciling on-chain wallet balances to client liabilities and proving segregation, the control that separates a credible exchange from the next collapse.”

An exchange must prove it holds client assets 1:1 and keeps them segregated from house funds. Risk/finance must reconcile on-chain reserves to internal liabilities and produce an attestation.

Approach

  1. Enumerate the exchange's hot and cold wallets and pull live on-chain balances per asset.
  2. Aggregate client liabilities from the internal ledger and build a Merkle tree of user balances.
  3. Reconcile total reserves vs total liabilities per asset and compute the coverage ratio.
  4. Verify house-vs-client segregation and flag any commingling or shortfall.
  5. Generate a proof-of-reserves report with Merkle root for user-verifiable inclusion.

Stack: Python · Web3 / explorer APIs · Merkle-tree implementation · SQL · Plotly

Talk track: I reconciled on-chain reserves against ledger liabilities per asset and wrapped user balances in a Merkle tree so any customer can verify inclusion without exposing the whole book. The coverage ratio has to sit at or above 100% per asset, and the segregation check flags any client funds sitting in house wallets — the exact failure mode that sank FTX.

  • Reserve-vs-liability coverage ratio per asset
  • Segregation breaches / commingling flags
  • Merkle root & inclusion-proof coverage
  • Cold-vs-hot custody split

OTC Counterparty Credit Line & DvP Settlement Risk Manager

“I built the OTC desk's credit and settlement control — sizing counterparty credit lines and enforcing delivery-versus-payment so a large bilateral trade can't turn into a settlement loss.”

Crypto OTC settles bilaterally without a CCP, so a counterparty can fail after you've paid. Risk must set credit lines, require pre-funding where needed, and control settlement (DvP) risk.

Approach

  1. Score OTC counterparties and set credit lines by tier, tenor and product.
  2. Monitor real-time line utilisation across open trades and pre-settlement exposure.
  3. Model settlement risk for bilateral trades and enforce DvP / atomic-settlement or pre-funding rules.
  4. Stress a counterparty default at settlement and quantify replacement-cost loss.
  5. Trigger escalations (reduce line, require collateral/pre-fund) as utilisation or risk rises.

Stack: Python · pandas / numpy · Exchange / OTC booking data · Plotly

Talk track: Each counterparty gets a tiered credit line, and the monitor tracks pre-settlement and settlement exposure against it in real time. For large tickets I enforce DvP or pre-funding so we never deliver first into an unsecured promise. Stressing a default right at settlement quantified the replacement-cost hit, which justified tightening lines on two lower-tier names.

  • Pre-settlement vs settlement exposure
  • Settlement-default replacement-cost loss
  • % of flow settled DvP / pre-funded

Derivatives Liquidation Engine & Auto-Deleveraging Stress

“I modelled an exchange's liquidation engine end to end — margin breach, liquidation, insurance-fund drawdown and auto-deleveraging — to find where a big move forces socialised losses.”

A derivatives exchange must liquidate under-margined positions without bankrupting the insurance fund or resorting to auto-deleveraging (ADL). Risk must stress the waterfall under a large move.

Approach

  1. Model a book of leveraged positions with maintenance-margin and liquidation thresholds.
  2. Simulate a price shock that pushes positions into liquidation and route them to the liquidation engine.
  3. Model market-impact of liquidations and top-ups/drawdowns of the insurance fund.
  4. Trigger auto-deleveraging when the insurance fund is exhausted and identify ADL'd counterparties.
  5. Report insurance-fund survival, bankruptcy losses and ADL incidence across shock sizes.

Stack: Python · numpy / pandas · Order-book / impact model · Plotly

Talk track: I pushed the book through escalating price shocks and watched the waterfall: liquidations first, then insurance-fund drawdown, then ADL. Around a 25% gap with clustered leverage the fund emptied and ADL kicked in — socialising losses onto profitable traders, which is reputational poison. The output tells the exchange where to set maintenance margin and how large the insurance fund really needs to be.

  • Insurance-fund drawdown vs shock size
  • Bankruptcy (uncovered) losses
  • ADL incidence and affected accounts
  • Liquidation market-impact slippage
