Traditional Finance — Sell-Side Risk

Measuring, capitalising and controlling market and counterparty risk on trading desks at banks, dealers and broker-dealers.

Sell-side risk management lives inside the banks and broker-dealers that intermediate markets — the institutions that make prices, warehouse inventory, extend financing and structure derivatives so that the buy-side and corporates can transfer risk. Where the buy-side owns a portfolio outcome, the sell-side owns the plumbing and the balance sheet: a trading desk quotes two-sided markets, holds transient and structural inventory, runs large books of OTC and cleared derivatives, and must remain solvent, liquid and well-capitalised through the most extreme market conditions. The risk function is the independent second line that measures market and counterparty exposure, sizes and enforces limits, calculates and defends regulatory capital, and evidences to the board and to the regulator that the firm can absorb the losses its business generates.

The discipline is unusually quantitative and capital-centric. Market risk teams measure sensitivities (delta, vega, gamma, curvature), Value-at-Risk and Expected Shortfall, and run the Fundamental Review of the Trading Book (FRTB) machinery that determines how much capital each desk consumes. Counterparty credit risk teams model potential future exposure (PFE), expected exposure and effective EPE, compute exposure-at-default under SA-CCR or the Internal Model Method, and run the XVA desk that prices CVA, FVA and KVA into every trade. Traded-risk control sits at the intersection of the front office, finance, treasury and technology — every limit, every capital number and every margin call has a direct profit-and-loss and balance-sheet consequence, so the risk manager must be as fluent in the mechanics of a swap or a repo as in the regulation that governs it.

In Singapore, sell-side traded risk is shaped by the Monetary Authority of Singapore (MAS) capital, liquidity and risk-management framework — principally MAS Notice 637 (capital adequacy, implementing Basel III/IV including the FRTB market-risk and SA-CVA rules), MAS Notice 639 (large exposures), MAS Notices 649 and 652 (the Liquidity Coverage Ratio and Net Stable Funding Ratio), the Banking Act, the Securities and Futures Act OTC derivatives reporting and clearing regime, the Technology Risk Management and Outsourcing Guidelines, the annual Industry-Wide Stress Test (IWST), and recovery and resolution planning (RRP) expectations for significant banks. A credible sell-side risk professional in Singapore fluently connects the global Basel/FRTB/SA-CCR standards and their EU (CRR/CRD), US (Federal Reserve/OCC, Dodd-Frank Title VII, CCAR) and UK (PRA) implementations to the exact MAS Notice numbers, and can turn that regulation into the daily reality of running desks, limits and capital.

Key regulatory frameworks (MAS & statutes)

MAS Notice 637 — Capital Adequacy (Basel III/IV, FRTB Market Risk & SA-CVA)

Monetary Authority of Singapore (MAS) · In force (Basel III/IV reforms and FRTB phasing in from 2024-2025)

+

MAS Notice 637 is the Singapore implementation of the Basel capital framework for locally-incorporated banks. It prescribes minimum Common Equity Tier 1, Tier 1 and Total capital ratios, the capital conservation and countercyclical buffers, and the risk-weighted-asset methodologies for credit, market, counterparty credit, CVA and operational risk — including the Fundamental Review of the Trading Book (FRTB) standardised and internal-models approaches for market risk and the standardised approach for CVA (SA-CVA).

Key requirements

  • Maintain CET1, Tier 1 and Total capital ratios above the prescribed minimums plus capital conservation and countercyclical buffers (MAS sets higher-than-Basel minimums for Singapore-incorporated banks).
  • Compute market-risk RWA under FRTB — either the Standardised Approach (sensitivities-based method, default risk charge and residual risk add-on) or, with MAS approval, the Internal Models Approach using Expected Shortfall and non-modellable risk factors.
  • Enforce the trading-book / banking-book boundary and evidence desk structure, risk-factor eligibility and P&L attribution (PLA) testing for IMA desks.
  • Calculate counterparty credit-risk RWA (SA-CCR or IMM) and CVA capital (SA-CVA / BA-CVA), and hold operational-risk capital under the standardised approach.

Implications for risk

  • Every trading desk consumes regulatory capital, so risk sizing has an explicit RWA and return-on-capital cost the front office must respect.
  • FRTB desk structure, risk-factor modellability and PLA/backtesting outcomes directly determine whether a desk keeps internal-model approval or reverts to the more punitive standardised charge.
  • Capital planning, RWA forecasting and the CVA/counterparty capital number become recurring risk deliverables reviewed by MAS.

References

  • · MAS Notice 637 — Notice on Risk Based Capital Adequacy Requirements for Banks Incorporated in Singapore
  • · BCBS — Minimum capital requirements for market risk (FRTB, d457)

MAS Notice 639 — Exposures to Single Counterparty Groups (Large Exposures)

Monetary Authority of Singapore (MAS) · In force

+

MAS Notice 639 implements the Basel large-exposures framework, limiting a bank's exposure to any single counterparty or group of connected counterparties to a percentage of its eligible capital base, so that the failure of one counterparty cannot threaten the bank's solvency. Trading-book and derivative exposures (measured via SA-CCR) count toward these limits.

Key requirements

  • Cap aggregate exposure to a single counterparty / connected group at the prescribed percentage of eligible (Tier 1) capital.
  • Aggregate on- and off-balance-sheet exposures, including SA-CCR derivative exposure, securities financing and settlement exposure, across the counterparty group.
  • Identify connected counterparties (control and economic interdependence) and apply tighter limits to exposures to other systemically important institutions.

Implications for risk

  • Counterparty exposure aggregation must span every product and desk, linking market-risk and counterparty-credit systems into a single group view.
  • Concentration limits sit alongside market-risk and VaR limits and can constrain desk activity even when market risk is within appetite.

References

  • · MAS Notice 639 — Exposures to Single Counterparty Groups
  • · BCBS — Supervisory framework for measuring and controlling large exposures

MAS Notices 649 (LCR) & 652 (NSFR) — Liquidity Standards

Monetary Authority of Singapore (MAS) · In force

+

MAS Notice 649 implements the Basel Liquidity Coverage Ratio, requiring banks to hold enough high-quality liquid assets (HQLA) to survive a 30-day stress, while MAS Notice 652 implements the Net Stable Funding Ratio, requiring stable funding to be matched to the liquidity profile of assets over a one-year horizon. Both directly shape how trading inventory, derivatives margin and repo books are funded.

Key requirements

  • Maintain an LCR of at least 100% — a stock of HQLA sufficient to cover net cash outflows over a 30-calendar-day stress scenario.
  • Maintain an NSFR of at least 100% — available stable funding at or above required stable funding for the asset base.
  • Include derivative collateral flows, potential margin calls, and secured/unsecured funding run-off in the stress calculations.

Implications for risk

  • Trading inventory and derivative margin consume HQLA and stable funding, so liquidity cost must be attributed to desks alongside capital.
  • Contingent margin outflows under a market shock link market risk directly to funding-liquidity risk and the LCR.

References

  • · MAS Notice 649 — Minimum Liquid Assets and Liquidity Coverage Ratio
  • · MAS Notice 652 — Net Stable Funding Ratio

Banking Act & SFA OTC Derivatives Reporting and Clearing

Monetary Authority of Singapore (MAS) · In force (Banking Act; SFA OTC derivatives regime phased)

+

The Banking Act is the primary statute governing banks in Singapore, empowering MAS to set prudential requirements and take supervisory action, while the Securities and Futures Act (SFA) OTC derivatives regime mandates reporting of OTC derivative contracts to a licensed trade repository and central clearing of prescribed products, mirroring the G20 post-crisis derivatives reforms.

Key requirements

  • Comply with Banking Act prudential requirements, licensing conditions and MAS directions, and maintain sound governance and risk management.
  • Report specified OTC derivative contracts (interest rate, credit, FX, commodity, equity) to a licensed/recognised trade repository with required data fields.
  • Centrally clear prescribed classes of standardised OTC derivatives (e.g. certain interest-rate swaps) through approved/recognised clearing facilities where mandated.

Implications for risk

  • Central clearing shifts bilateral counterparty risk to CCPs and converts it into margin and default-fund / liquidity risk that must be modelled.
  • Trade-reporting data quality relies on the same clean position and exposure data the risk function depends on internally.

References

  • · Singapore — Banking Act 1970
  • · MAS — Securities and Futures (Reporting of Derivatives Contracts) Regulations and clearing mandates

MAS Technology Risk Management & Outsourcing Guidelines

Monetary Authority of Singapore (MAS) · In force

+

The Technology Risk Management (TRM) Guidelines set expectations for system resilience, cyber security, access and change management and incident response, while the Outsourcing Guidelines govern reliance on third-party and cloud providers — both critical to trading and risk platforms that must revalue books, run capital engines and process margin without failure.

Key requirements

  • Ensure availability, capacity and resilience of critical trading, pricing, risk and settlement systems, with tested recovery objectives.
  • Implement access control, change management, cyber-defence, penetration testing and incident-response processes per the TRM Guidelines.
  • Perform due diligence, ongoing monitoring, business-continuity, exit and concentration-risk management for material outsourcing and cloud arrangements, retaining accountability.

Implications for risk

  • A failed overnight risk or capital batch, or a pricing-system outage, becomes an operational risk that can crystallise as un-hedged market risk or a capital-reporting breach.
  • Vendor and cloud concentration (single risk-engine or market-data provider) becomes a monitored operational-risk exposure.

References

  • · MAS — Technology Risk Management Guidelines
  • · MAS — Guidelines on Outsourcing

Industry-Wide Stress Test (IWST), Recovery & Resolution Planning (RRP)

Monetary Authority of Singapore (MAS) · In force (annual IWST; RRP for D-SIBs and significant banks)

+

MAS runs an annual Industry-Wide Stress Test in which banks assess capital and liquidity resilience against common macro-financial and market scenarios, complemented by firm-specific reverse stress testing. Domestic systemically important banks and other significant institutions must maintain recovery and resolution plans setting out how they would restore viability or be resolved in an orderly way.

Key requirements

  • Project P&L, RWA, capital and liquidity ratios under MAS-prescribed macro and market stress scenarios (rates, spreads, equities, FX, property) and report results to MAS.
  • Perform reverse stress testing to identify scenarios that would render the business model non-viable.
  • Maintain recovery plans (credible recovery options and triggers) and provide the information needed for resolution planning, including critical-functions and continuity analysis.

Implications for risk

  • Stress-scenario P&L on the trading and derivatives books is a central risk deliverable feeding capital adequacy and recovery triggers.
  • Reverse stress testing and RRP force the risk function to link market and counterparty losses to solvency, funding and orderly-wind-down mechanics.

References

  • · MAS — Industry-Wide Stress Test exercises and scenarios
  • · MAS — Recovery and Resolution Planning requirements for banks

World

Global frameworks market participants abide by

Basel III / IV & the Fundamental Review of the Trading Book (FRTB)

Basel Committee on Banking Supervision (BCBS) · Finalised (Basel III endgame / IV); FRTB implementation from 2023-2025 by jurisdiction

+

The Basel III/IV reforms overhaul trading-book capital via FRTB — a stricter trading/banking-book boundary, a sensitivities-based Standardised Approach, an Expected-Shortfall Internal Models Approach with non-modellable risk factors and desk-level P&L-attribution and backtesting, plus revised credit, CVA and operational-risk frameworks and an aggregate output floor.

Key requirements

  • Adopt FRTB-SA or FRTB-IMA for market-risk capital, with a hard trading/banking-book boundary and desk-level model approval.
  • For IMA, replace 99% VaR with 97.5% Expected Shortfall calibrated to a stress period, add capital for non-modellable risk factors (NMRF), and pass PLA and backtesting.
  • Apply the revised standardised approaches and the output floor limiting internal-model benefit to a percentage of standardised RWA.

Implications for risk

  • The global benchmark that MAS Notice 637 localises — its concepts (ES, NMRF, PLA, desk structure) are the daily vocabulary of sell-side market risk.
  • The output floor and NMRF charges materially raise capital for illiquid and exotic products, reshaping desk viability.

References

  • · BCBS — Minimum capital requirements for market risk (FRTB)
  • · BCBS — Basel III: Finalising post-crisis reforms

SA-CCR & CVA Capital Framework

Basel Committee on Banking Supervision (BCBS) · In force (SA-CCR; revised CVA framework implementation from 2024-2025)

+

SA-CCR is the standardised approach for measuring counterparty credit-risk exposure at default on derivatives, replacing the Current Exposure Method with a risk-sensitive replacement-cost-plus-potential-future-exposure formula. The revised CVA framework (BA-CVA / SA-CVA) capitalises the risk of mark-to-market losses from counterparty credit-spread and exposure moves.

Key requirements

  • Compute EAD under SA-CCR as alpha × (replacement cost + PFE add-on) with asset-class hedging sets, supervisory factors and margining recognition.
  • Hold CVA capital under BA-CVA or, subject to approval, SA-CVA which recognises eligible CVA hedges and sensitivities.
  • Recognise netting and collateral (CSA) terms consistently across exposure, capital and large-exposure calculations.

Implications for risk

  • SA-CCR EAD feeds counterparty capital, large-exposure limits and leverage-ratio exposure simultaneously.
  • CVA capital gives the XVA desk an explicit hedging mandate and links counterparty spread risk to market-risk management.

References

  • · BCBS — SA-CCR: The standardised approach for measuring counterparty credit risk exposures
  • · BCBS — Credit valuation adjustment risk: targeted final revisions

EU Capital Requirements Regulation/Directive (CRR/CRD) & EMIR

European Union / European Banking Authority (EBA) / ESMA · In force (CRR3/CRD6 implementing Basel IV/FRTB; EMIR / EMIR REFIT)

+

The CRR and CRD are the EU implementation of Basel, with CRR3/CRD6 bringing in FRTB, SA-CCR, the revised CVA framework and the output floor, while the European Market Infrastructure Regulation (EMIR) governs OTC derivative clearing, reporting and risk-mitigation (including margin for uncleared derivatives).

Key requirements

  • Apply CRR capital, leverage, large-exposure and FRTB market-risk rules across EU-supervised entities.
  • Clear mandated OTC derivative classes through authorised CCPs and report all derivatives to trade repositories under EMIR.
  • Exchange initial and variation margin for uncleared derivatives and apply EMIR risk-mitigation techniques (confirmation, reconciliation, dispute resolution).

Implications for risk

  • Global banks must reconcile MAS, EU and other capital numbers for the same books, so methodology consistency is a risk-control concern.
  • EMIR clearing and margin rules shape counterparty exposure, collateral and funding for EU-facing trading.

References

  • · EU — Capital Requirements Regulation (CRR) / Directive (CRD), including CRR3/CRD6
  • · EU — European Market Infrastructure Regulation (EMIR) and EMIR REFIT

US Dodd-Frank Title VII & Federal Reserve / OCC CCAR Stress Testing

US Federal Reserve / OCC / SEC / CFTC · In force (Dodd-Frank 2010; annual CCAR/DFAST cycles)

+

Title VII of the Dodd-Frank Act reshaped OTC derivatives with mandatory clearing, swap execution, trade reporting, margin and swap-dealer registration, while the Federal Reserve's Comprehensive Capital Analysis and Review (CCAR) and Dodd-Frank Act Stress Tests (DFAST) impose severe supervisory stress scenarios (including a Global Market Shock for trading firms) that drive capital planning.

Key requirements

  • Clear standardised swaps through CCPs, execute on SEFs where required, and report swaps to registered repositories; register as a swap dealer where in scope.
  • Project capital under supervisory baseline and severely adverse scenarios; large trading firms apply the Global Market Shock and counterparty-default components.
  • Meet post-stress minimum capital ratios and incorporate results into the stress capital buffer.

Implications for risk

  • The CCAR Global Market Shock is an instantaneous, severe re-pricing of trading positions — a defining sell-side market-risk stress exercise.
  • Title VII margin and clearing rules interlock with SA-CCR exposure and funding-liquidity management.

References

  • · US — Dodd-Frank Wall Street Reform and Consumer Protection Act, Title VII
  • · Federal Reserve — Comprehensive Capital Analysis and Review (CCAR) and Dodd-Frank Act Stress Test (DFAST)

UK Prudential Regulation Authority (PRA) Framework

Bank of England / Prudential Regulation Authority (PRA) · In force (Basel 3.1 UK implementation; annual concurrent stress tests)

+

Post-Brexit, the PRA sets UK bank capital, liquidity and market-risk rules through its Rulebook and Basel 3.1 implementation (including FRTB and SA-CCR), supervises internal-model approvals, and runs annual concurrent stress tests calibrated to UK macro-financial and traded-risk scenarios.

Key requirements

  • Comply with PRA capital, leverage and liquidity requirements and Basel 3.1 market-risk (FRTB) and counterparty (SA-CCR) rules.
  • Obtain and maintain PRA model permissions for IMA/IMM, subject to model-risk management (SS1/23) expectations.
  • Participate in PRA concurrent stress tests, including traded-risk and counterparty-credit stress components.

Implications for risk

  • Global dealers must maintain PRA-compliant models and stress results alongside MAS, EU and US equivalents.
  • PRA model-risk-management expectations raise the bar for validation and governance of VaR/ES, SA-CCR and XVA models.

References

  • · PRA — Rulebook and Basel 3.1 (near-final) market-risk and CCR policy
  • · PRA / Bank of England — Annual Cyclical Scenario stress tests; SS1/23 Model risk management

G-SIB Framework & Leverage Ratio

Basel Committee on Banking Supervision (BCBS) / Financial Stability Board (FSB) · In force (annual G-SIB assessment; leverage-ratio requirements)

+

The global systemically important bank (G-SIB) framework scores banks on size, interconnectedness, cross-jurisdictional activity, substitutability and complexity, assigning higher-loss-absorbency (HLA) capital buffers and TLAC requirements, while the Basel leverage ratio caps total (non-risk-weighted) exposure — with derivative exposure measured via SA-CCR — relative to Tier 1 capital.

Key requirements

  • Report the G-SIB indicators annually; higher-bucket G-SIBs hold additional CET1 HLA buffers and meet TLAC/MREL.
  • Maintain a leverage ratio above the minimum, with SA-CCR-based derivative and SFT exposure in the denominator.
  • Manage complexity and interconnectedness indicators (OTC derivatives notional, Level 3 assets, cross-jurisdictional claims) that drive the surcharge.

Implications for risk

  • Large derivative and trading books increase G-SIB indicators and leverage exposure, so balance-sheet and notional efficiency become risk-managed metrics.
  • The leverage ratio can bind before risk-based capital, constraining low-risk-weight but large-exposure businesses like repo and cleared derivatives.

References

  • · BCBS — Global systemically important banks: assessment methodology and higher loss absorbency
  • · BCBS — Basel III leverage ratio framework; FSB — Total Loss-Absorbing Capacity (TLAC)

FRTB Standardised Approach

FRTB-SAMarket Risk Capital
Open demo →

What it is. The Basel-mandated standardised method for computing market-risk capital, built from a sensitivities-based method (delta, vega, curvature across risk classes), a default risk charge (DRC) and a residual risk add-on (RRAO). It is both the fallback for desks without model approval and the benchmark for the output floor.

When used. Capital reporting for all trading desks, the floor against internal-model capital, new-product and desk viability analysis, and jurisdictions/desks where IMA is not sought or is revoked.

Methodology. Compute delta, vega and curvature sensitivities per risk factor; bucket them and aggregate within and across buckets using prescribed risk weights and correlations under three correlation scenarios (high/medium/low). Add the DRC for jump-to-default and the RRAO for exotic/residual risks; sum the components to the SA capital charge.

Inputs / outputs. Inputs: standardised sensitivities (GIRR, CSR, equity, FX, commodity), jump-to-default exposures, notionals for residual risks. Outputs: SBM capital by risk class and bucket, DRC, RRAO, and total SA charge (also used for the output floor).

Interview talking points

  • Walk through the sensitivities-based method: delta/vega/curvature, buckets, risk weights and the three correlation scenarios.
  • Explain the default risk charge and residual risk add-on and when each bites (e.g. exotics, gap risk).
  • Discuss why FRTB-SA is more risk-sensitive than the old standardised rules and how it anchors the output floor.

Skills: Sensitivities / greeks · Regulatory capital · Python / data engineering

FRTB Internal Models Approach

FRTB-IMAMarket Risk Capital
Open demo →

What it is. The model-based FRTB approach that replaces 99% VaR with a stressed-calibrated 97.5% Expected Shortfall, adds capital for non-modellable risk factors (NMRF), and requires desk-level P&L-attribution (PLA) and backtesting to keep model approval.

When used. Capital for approved desks with rich, liquid risk factors where the ES-based charge is materially lower than SA; ongoing model governance, PLA and backtesting cycles.

Methodology. Compute Expected Shortfall at 97.5% over a stress period with liquidity-horizon scaling by risk-factor class; identify modellable vs. non-modellable risk factors (real-price observability test) and add stressed NMRF capital; run daily backtesting and quarterly PLA (Spearman correlation and Kolmogorov-Smirnov metrics) at desk level, with a default-risk-charge model for issuer default.

Inputs / outputs. Inputs: full risk-factor time series, hypothetical/actual/risk-theoretical P&L, liquidity horizons, risk-factor eligibility data. Outputs: ES capital, NMRF add-on, DRC, PLA/backtesting status, and desk model-approval traffic lights.

Interview talking points

  • Explain the shift from VaR to Expected Shortfall and liquidity-horizon scaling under FRTB.
  • Describe NMRF: the real-price observability test and why illiquid factors are penalised.
  • Detail PLA (the desk test using RTPL vs. HPL) and how a desk fails into the standardised charge.

Skills: Expected Shortfall / statistics · Model validation · Time-series data

Value-at-Risk & Expected Shortfall

VaR / ESMarket Risk Measurement
Open demo →

What it is. The core market-risk metrics: VaR estimates the loss threshold at a confidence level over a horizon, and Expected Shortfall (a coherent measure) gives the average loss beyond that threshold — used for internal limits, management reporting and, in ES form, FRTB capital.

When used. Daily desk and firm risk reporting, limit setting and monitoring, backtesting and model validation, and management/board risk communication.

Methodology. Historical simulation (full revaluation or sensitivity-based over a return window), parametric/variance-covariance, and Monte Carlo. ES averages the tail beyond the VaR quantile. Both require clean risk-factor mapping, decisions on weighting/decay and horizon scaling, and regular backtesting (Kupiec, traffic-light) against clean P&L.

Inputs / outputs. Inputs: positions, risk-factor sensitivities, historical returns/vols/correlations, confidence and horizon. Outputs: VaR/ES, marginal/component contributions by desk and factor, and backtesting exceptions.

Interview talking points

  • Explain why 99% VaR is silent on tail severity, motivating ES and its sub-additivity (coherence).
  • Contrast historical, parametric and Monte Carlo VaR and their trade-offs.
  • Describe backtesting (Kupiec / Basel traffic-light) and how exceptions trigger multiplier increases or model review.

Skills: Statistics and distributions · Python / pandas / numpy · Risk-factor mapping

Standardised Approach for Counterparty Credit Risk

SA-CCRCounterparty Credit Risk
Open demo →

What it is. The Basel standardised method for measuring derivative exposure-at-default, computed as alpha × (replacement cost + potential future exposure add-on), replacing the Current Exposure Method with a more risk-sensitive, netting- and margin-aware formula.

When used. Counterparty capital (RWA), large-exposure and leverage-ratio exposure measurement, and pre-trade counterparty exposure/limits for firms not on IMM.

Methodology. For each netting set, compute replacement cost (current MtM net of collateral, with margined vs. unmargined treatment) and a PFE add-on aggregated across asset-class hedging sets using supervisory factors, deltas and maturity factors; multiply the sum by alpha (1.4). Recognises netting and CSA collateral with a multiplier for over-collateralisation.

Inputs / outputs. Inputs: trade-level MtM, asset class, notionals/maturities, supervisory deltas, netting and CSA/collateral terms. Outputs: EAD per netting set, feeding RWA, CVA, large-exposure and leverage calculations.

Interview talking points

  • Break down EAD = alpha × (RC + PFE) and the difference between margined and unmargined netting sets.
  • Explain hedging sets, supervisory factors and the collateral/over-collateralisation multiplier.
  • Contrast SA-CCR with the old CEM and with IMM, and why SA-CCR feeds multiple ratios at once.

Skills: Derivatives mechanics · Netting / ISDA-CSA · Regulatory capital

Internal Model Method — PFE / EEPE / EAD

IMM (PFE/EEPE/EAD)Counterparty Credit Risk
Open demo →

What it is. The simulation-based approach (subject to regulatory approval) for counterparty exposure: Monte-Carlo the evolution of exposures to produce the exposure profile, from which Potential Future Exposure, Expected Exposure, Effective EPE and the exposure-at-default (EAD = alpha × Effective EPE) are derived.

When used. Counterparty capital and limits at large dealers with model approval, XVA exposure profiles, wrong-way-risk analysis, and pre-deal counterparty limit checks.

Methodology. Simulate correlated risk-factor paths over many time steps; revalue the netting set (with collateral and margin-period-of-risk modelling) along each path to build the exposure distribution; extract PFE (a high percentile), Expected Exposure and its time profile, then Effective EPE (non-decreasing average EE) and EAD. Incorporate wrong-way risk and collateral dynamics.

Inputs / outputs. Inputs: risk-factor models and correlations, trade pricers, netting/CSA terms, margin period of risk. Outputs: PFE profile, EE/EEPE, EAD, peak exposure and wrong-way-risk metrics feeding capital, limits and XVA.

Interview talking points

  • Define PFE, EE, EPE and Effective EPE and how EAD = alpha × Effective EPE follows.
  • Explain the margin period of risk and how collateral and thresholds reshape the exposure profile.
  • Discuss wrong-way risk (general vs. specific) and how it inflates exposure and CVA.

Skills: Monte Carlo simulation · Derivatives pricing · Model validation

Valuation Adjustments

XVADerivatives Valuation / Counterparty Risk
Open demo →

What it is. The family of pricing adjustments that move a derivative from a risk-free value to its true economic value: CVA (counterparty default), DVA (own default), FVA (funding), ColVA, KVA (capital) and MVA (initial-margin funding) — owned by a dedicated XVA desk.

When used. Pricing new trades, managing and hedging counterparty and funding risk, computing accounting CVA/DVA, and steering trades toward capital- and funding-efficient structures.

Methodology. Use IMM/Monte-Carlo expected-exposure profiles combined with counterparty credit curves (PD/LGD) for CVA/DVA, funding curves for FVA/MVA, and projected regulatory capital for KVA; integrate over the exposure profile and hedge the resulting sensitivities (credit spread, rates, FX) in the market.

Inputs / outputs. Inputs: EE/EPE profiles, CDS/credit curves, funding and discount curves, capital projections, netting/CSA terms. Outputs: CVA/DVA/FVA/KVA/MVA values and Greeks, incremental trade XVA, and hedging requirements.

Interview talking points

  • Define each XVA and explain how CVA is essentially the price of a contingent option on counterparty default.
  • Discuss FVA controversy and the funding-vs-valuation debate, and how KVA prices the lifetime cost of capital.
  • Explain how the CVA desk hedges credit-spread and exposure risk and links to SA-CVA capital.

Skills: Derivatives pricing · Credit / funding curves · Monte Carlo / numerics

ISDA SIMM & Uncleared Margin Rules

SIMM / UMR (IM/VM)Margin / Counterparty Risk
Open demo →

What it is. The Uncleared Margin Rules require in-scope counterparties to exchange variation margin daily and segregated initial margin bilaterally; the ISDA Standard Initial Margin Model (SIMM) is the industry-standard, sensitivity-based methodology used to compute that regulatory initial margin.

When used. Daily VM and IM exchange for non-centrally-cleared derivatives, IM forecasting and MVA, CSA/collateral optimisation, and dispute avoidance between counterparties.

Methodology. VM tracks daily mark-to-market. SIMM computes IM from delta, vega and curvature sensitivities (in CRIF format) across risk classes (rates/FX, credit, equity, commodity), applying prescribed risk weights and correlations to a 10-day, 99% margin standard — designed to be transparent and replicable so counterparties agree on the number.

Inputs / outputs. Inputs: standardised CRIF sensitivities, product/risk-class classification, netting-set and CSA terms. Outputs: initial margin by netting set and risk class, IM forecasts for MVA, and reconciliation/dispute data.

Interview talking points

  • Explain UMR phase-in by AANA threshold and the difference between VM and segregated IM.
  • Walk through SIMM's sensitivity-based, CRIF-driven calculation and why an industry-standard model reduces disputes.
  • Link IM to funding cost (MVA) and to collateral optimisation across CSAs.

Skills: Sensitivities / CRIF · Collateral management · ISDA-CSA documentation

Trading Limit Frameworks

Risk Governance / Control
Open demo →

What it is. The structured hierarchy of limits — VaR/ES, sensitivity (DV01, CS01, vega), stress-loss, notional, concentration, stop-loss and counterparty limits — that operationalises the board's risk appetite into enforceable desk-level controls with monitoring and escalation.

When used. Daily limit monitoring and breach management, risk-appetite cascade, new-desk/product onboarding, and pre-trade limit checks.

Methodology. Middle office

The middle office: flow, responsibilities & skills

On the sell-side, the middle office is the independent control layer between the trading desks (front office) and settlements, confirmations and the general ledger (back office). It produces and signs off the official daily P&L, verifies marks, generates risk and limit reporting, manages collateral, and supplies the position and sensitivity data that feeds regulatory capital. It is where a trader's intraday view is turned into a controlled, explained and reconciled firm number — and where most desk-to-finance disputes are resolved.

Assemble FRTB and SA-CCR inputs, assure trade-reporting completeness (SFA/EMIR/Dodd-Frank), and reconcile front-office risk to finance capital numbers.

Core responsibilities

  • Validate trade capture and manage the lifecycle of amendments, novations and exercises.
  • Own independent price verification, reserves and prudent-valuation adjustments.
  • Generate market-risk, counterparty and stress reporting and monitor the limit framework.
  • Manage bilateral and cleared collateral, margin calls, disputes and SIMM reconciliation.
  • Supply and reconcile the position/sensitivity data underpinning regulatory capital and trade reporting.
  • Act as the independent control bridge between trading, finance, treasury and operations.

Workflow

What a day looks like

Typical roles: Market Risk Analyst / Manager (traded risk) · Counterparty Credit Risk Analyst · XVA / CVA Desk Quant or Risk Manager · FRTB / Regulatory Capital Specialist · Chief Risk Officer / Head of Traded Risk at a bank or dealer

Morning

  • Run and validate the overnight risk and capital batch — VaR/ES, sensitivities, SA-CCR/IMM exposure and FRTB RWA across all desks.
  • Review limit breaches, backtesting exceptions and P&L attribution (clean vs. dirty P&L) for anomalies.
  • Check overnight market moves and their impact on the largest positions, counterparty exposures and CVA.
  • Confirm margin calls (VM/IM), collateral movements and CCP margin settled, and reconcile counterparty exposure.

Midday

  • Provide pre-trade what-if analysis — VaR, capital (FRTB/SA-CCR), limit and XVA impact — for large or structured trades.
  • Challenge concentrated, illiquid or capital-intensive positions with the front office and discuss RWA/return-on-capital.
  • Progress FRTB desk-level PLA/backtesting, NMRF analysis and model-validation work with quants.
  • Review SA-CCR/IMM exposure and CVA hedging with the XVA desk and update counterparty limits.

Evening

  • Prepare daily and weekly risk packs for the traded-risk committee, CRO and desk heads.
  • Sign off end-of-day limit monitoring and document breaches, escalation and remediation.
  • Perform independent price verification (IPV) and prudent-valuation checks on illiquid or hard-to-mark positions.
  • Progress regulatory deliverables — MAS Notice 637 capital, large-exposure (639) and IWST stress submissions.

Crisis mode

  • Stand up intraday risk, exposure and margin monitoring; recompute VaR, stress and counterparty exposure as markets move.
  • Model a market shock (Global-Market-Shock style) and a major counterparty default across the derivatives book.
  • Coordinate with treasury on collateral, funding-liquidity (LCR) and CCP margin under stress.
  • Escalate to the CRO and risk committee and, where required, brief MAS on material capital, liquidity or exposure issues.

Stakeholders: Trading desk heads and traders (front office) · Chief Risk Officer and traded-risk / board risk committee · Finance / product control, treasury and collateral management · XVA desk, quants and model validation · Compliance, legal (ISDA/CSA) and regulatory reporting · CCPs, prime brokers, OTC counterparties and MAS / other regulators
