TradFi Buy-Side

Traditional Finance — Buy-Side Risk

Protecting capital across multi-asset portfolios at asset managers, hedge funds, insurers and sovereign funds.

Buy-side risk management sits inside the institutions that own and allocate capital — asset managers, hedge funds, pension and sovereign wealth funds, insurers and family offices. Unlike sell-side desks that intermediate and warehouse risk for clients, the buy-side owns the portfolio outcome: the mandate is to generate risk-adjusted returns within an explicitly agreed risk budget, liquidity profile and regulatory perimeter. The risk function is the independent second line that challenges the front office, sizes and enforces limits, and evidences to boards, clients and regulators that risk is understood before it is taken.

The discipline spans market risk (rates, credit spread, equity, FX and commodity exposures measured through VaR, expected shortfall, sensitivities and scenario analysis), liquidity risk (both funding liquidity — can we meet redemptions and margin calls — and asset liquidity — can we exit positions without moving the market), counterparty and credit risk (bilateral OTC exposure, CSA collateral terms, prime broker and custodian concentration), and operational and model risk. On the buy-side these are inseparable from investment mandates, client suitability, fund liquidity terms and the fiduciary duty owed to end investors.

In Singapore, buy-side risk is shaped by the Monetary Authority of Singapore (MAS) Securities and Futures Act licensing regime, the SFA and Financial Advisers Act conduct requirements, MAS Notices and Guidelines on risk management practices, and — for the fast-growing family office and fund ecosystem — the 13O/13U tax incentive conditions. A credible buy-side risk professional in Singapore fluently connects global standards (Basel-derived counterparty frameworks, UCITS/AIFMD-style liquidity thinking, IOSCO principles) to the MAS rulebook and to the practical realities of running money across Asian and global markets.

Singapore

Key regulatory frameworks (MAS & statutes)

Securities and Futures Act 2001 (SFA) & Capital Markets Services (CMS) Licensing

Monetary Authority of Singapore (MAS) · In force (SFA 2001, continuously updated)

+

The SFA is the primary statute governing capital markets activity in Singapore. Buy-side firms conducting regulated activities such as fund management or dealing in capital markets products must hold a Capital Markets Services (CMS) licence or be a Registered Fund Management Company (RFMC)/Licensed Fund Management Company (LFMC), each with defined AUM, client-type and base capital conditions.

Key requirements

  • Hold the appropriate CMS licence for fund management and appoint fit-and-proper key individuals and representatives with the required competency.
  • Maintain base capital and risk-based capital / financial resources above prescribed minimums (e.g. base capital thresholds for LFMCs) and monitor them on an ongoing basis.
  • Segregate and safeguard customer assets, maintain proper books and records, and submit periodic returns to MAS.
  • Implement independent risk management and compliance functions proportionate to the nature, scale and complexity of the business.

Implications for risk

  • Risk teams must produce evidence that limits, valuation and liquidity controls are commensurate with licence conditions and AUM.
  • Capital and financial-resource monitoring becomes a daily/near-daily risk deliverable, not just a finance task.
  • Any material limit breach or control failure may be reportable and feeds fit-and-proper assessments of key individuals.

References

  • · MAS — Securities and Futures Act 2001
  • · MAS — Guidelines on Licensing, Registration and Conduct of Business for Fund Management Companies (SFA 04-G05)

MAS Notice on Risk Based Capital Adequacy & Financial Resources (CMS holders)

Monetary Authority of Singapore (MAS) · In force

+

MAS prescribes financial resources and risk-based capital requirements for CMS licence holders, requiring firms to hold capital against operational, position and counterparty risks and to maintain a financial resources ratio above the regulatory floor.

Key requirements

  • Compute and maintain a financial resources ratio (adjusted financial resources over total risk requirement) above the minimum.
  • Quantify operational risk requirement, position (market) risk requirement and counterparty risk requirement using prescribed methodologies.
  • Notify MAS promptly when the ratio approaches or breaches early-warning thresholds.

Implications for risk

  • Market and counterparty exposures directly consume regulatory capital, so risk sizing has an explicit capital cost the front office must respect.
  • Stress losses can erode financial resources, linking scenario analysis to solvency and continuity of the licence.

References

  • · MAS Notice SFA04-N13 — Risk Based Capital Adequacy Requirements for Holders of a CMS Licence
  • · MAS — Financial resources / notices for capital markets intermediaries

MAS Guidelines on Risk Management Practices (Board & Senior Management, Market, Liquidity, Credit)

Monetary Authority of Singapore (MAS) · In force (guidelines series)

+

MAS issues a suite of Risk Management Practices guidelines covering board and senior management oversight, market risk, liquidity risk, credit risk and internal controls. They set supervisory expectations for governance, independent risk oversight, measurement and reporting.

Key requirements

  • Establish an independent risk function with clear reporting lines to the board / risk committee, separate from revenue generation.
  • Define risk appetite and cascade it into limits (VaR, sensitivity, concentration, liquidity) with escalation and breach-management protocols.
  • Perform regular stress testing and scenario analysis, and maintain robust valuation and independent price verification (IPV).
  • Document policies, model validation and management information so oversight is evidenced.

Implications for risk

  • Risk appetite statements and limit frameworks must be board-approved and demonstrably enforced, not merely documented.
  • Independence of the risk function is a supervisory focus — segregation of duties and reporting lines are examined.
  • Stress testing and IPV are recurring deliverables reviewed by MAS during inspections.

References

  • · MAS — Guidelines on Risk Management Practices (Board and Senior Management; Market Risk; Liquidity Risk; Credit Risk; Internal Controls)

MAS Guidelines on Outsourcing & Technology / Cyber Risk Management

Monetary Authority of Singapore (MAS) · In force

+

MAS Guidelines on Outsourcing and the Technology Risk Management (TRM) Guidelines set expectations for managing risks arising from third-party service providers, cloud adoption, data governance and cyber resilience — increasingly relevant as buy-side firms rely on external administrators, custodians and analytics vendors.

Key requirements

  • Perform due diligence and ongoing monitoring of material outsourcing arrangements, with clear accountability retained by the firm.
  • Maintain business continuity, exit and concentration-risk plans for critical service providers.
  • Implement technology and cyber controls, access management, and incident response consistent with the TRM Guidelines.

Implications for risk

  • Operational and third-party risk feed the enterprise risk register and can crystallise into liquidity or valuation issues (e.g. administrator failure).
  • Vendor concentration (single fund administrator, single data provider) becomes a monitored risk exposure.

References

  • · MAS — Guidelines on Outsourcing
  • · MAS — Technology Risk Management Guidelines

MAS Notice 626 — AML/CFT & 13O/13U Family Office Framework

Monetary Authority of Singapore (MAS) · In force (MAS Notice 626; 13O/13U tax incentive conditions updated 2023)

+

AML/CFT obligations under MAS Notice 626 apply to capital markets intermediaries, while the 13O (Onshore Fund) and 13U (Enhanced Tier) income-tax incentive schemes govern the rapidly expanding Singapore single-family-office ecosystem, imposing minimum AUM, local investment, professional headcount and business-spending conditions.

Key requirements

  • Conduct customer due diligence, screening and ongoing monitoring; maintain risk-based AML/CFT programmes.
  • For 13O/13U vehicles, meet minimum fund size, local investing, investment professional and annual business-spend conditions to retain the incentive.
  • Maintain governance and substance in Singapore consistent with incentive and licensing expectations.

Implications for risk

  • Family-office growth in Singapore expands the buy-side risk remit into bespoke, concentrated and often illiquid portfolios.
  • Financial-crime and sanctions risk sit alongside market risk in the enterprise risk view for these vehicles.

References

  • · MAS Notice 626 — Prevention of Money Laundering and Countering the Financing of Terrorism
  • · MAS / Singapore Income Tax Act — Sections 13O and 13U fund incentive schemes

World

Global frameworks market participants abide by

Basel III / IV Counterparty Credit Risk (SA-CCR) & Margin for Uncleared Derivatives

Basel Committee on Banking Supervision (BCBS) / national regulators · In force, phasing (SA-CCR; UMR final phases from 2022)

+

Although Basel targets banks, its counterparty credit risk standard (SA-CCR) and the BCBS-IOSCO Uncleared Margin Rules (UMR) shape the terms on which buy-side firms trade OTC derivatives — driving initial and variation margin, CSA renegotiation and the economics of bilateral trading.

Key requirements

  • Post variation margin daily and, once in scope, exchange initial margin (segregated) for non-centrally cleared derivatives.
  • Manage exposure under SA-CCR-influenced pricing and capital charges passed through by dealers.
  • Maintain eligible collateral, haircuts and dispute-resolution processes under ISDA/CSA documentation.

Implications for risk

  • Margin and collateral obligations create funding-liquidity risk that must be forecast under stress.
  • IM/VM thresholds and CSA terms directly affect counterparty exposure and the cost of hedging.

References

  • · BCBS — SA-CCR: The standardised approach for measuring counterparty credit risk exposures
  • · BCBS-IOSCO — Margin requirements for non-centrally cleared derivatives (UMR)

UCITS & AIFMD (EU fund liquidity, leverage and risk governance)

European Securities and Markets Authority (ESMA) / EU · In force (UCITS Directive; AIFMD; AIFMD II adopted)

+

UCITS and the Alternative Investment Fund Managers Directive set the global reference point for fund-level risk governance: independent risk management, leverage limits and disclosure, liquidity risk management including stress testing, and eligible-asset and diversification rules.

Key requirements

  • Maintain a permanent, hierarchically independent risk management function within the manager.
  • Perform regular liquidity stress testing (LST) aligned with ESMA guidelines and maintain liquidity management tools (swing pricing, gates, redemption notice).
  • Measure and report leverage (commitment and, for AIFMD, gross/commitment methods) and calculate global exposure.

Implications for risk

  • Fund liquidity terms must be matched to underlying asset liquidity — a core buy-side risk deliverable.
  • Leverage and global-exposure measurement discipline transfers to non-EU mandates marketed to global investors.

References

  • · ESMA — Guidelines on liquidity stress testing in UCITS and AIFs
  • · EU — UCITS Directive; Alternative Investment Fund Managers Directive (AIFMD)

US Dodd-Frank Title VII & SEC/CFTC Derivatives and Reporting Rules

US SEC / CFTC · In force (Dodd-Frank 2010; ongoing rulemaking)

+

Title VII of Dodd-Frank reshaped OTC derivatives with mandatory clearing, swap execution, trade reporting and margin, while SEC/CFTC position reporting and Form PF drive risk transparency for private funds trading US markets.

Key requirements

  • Clear standardised swaps through CCPs where mandated and trade on SEFs where required.
  • Report swaps to trade repositories and comply with recordkeeping and position limits.
  • File Form PF / regulatory reporting on leverage, liquidity and counterparty exposures for in-scope private funds.

Implications for risk

  • Central clearing shifts bilateral counterparty risk to CCP and margin/liquidity risk.
  • Regulatory reporting requires the same clean exposure data the risk function relies on internally.

References

  • · US — Dodd-Frank Wall Street Reform and Consumer Protection Act, Title VII
  • · SEC/CFTC — Form PF; swap clearing, reporting and margin rules

IOSCO Principles & FSB Recommendations on Asset Management / Liquidity

IOSCO / Financial Stability Board (FSB) · In force (recommendations, periodically updated)

+

IOSCO and the FSB set global principles for open-ended fund liquidity mismatch, use of liquidity management tools, leverage measurement and operational resilience, forming the intellectual basis MAS and other regulators localise.

Key requirements

  • Align dealing frequency and redemption terms with the liquidity of the underlying portfolio.
  • Provide managers with a broad set of anti-dilution and quantity-based liquidity management tools.
  • Adopt consistent leverage metrics to allow supervisory comparison across funds.

Implications for risk

  • Provides the 'why' behind liquidity buckets, redemption modelling and swing pricing that risk teams build.
  • Supports defensible, standards-aligned methodology choices when challenged by clients or regulators.

References

  • · IOSCO — Recommendations for Liquidity Risk Management for Collective Investment Schemes
  • · FSB — Policy recommendations to address structural vulnerabilities from asset management activities

Tools & frameworks practitioners actually use

Value-at-Risk

VaRMarket Risk Measurement
Open demo →

What it is. A statistical estimate of the maximum expected loss on a portfolio over a defined horizon at a given confidence level (e.g. 1-day 99% VaR), summarising market risk in a single, comparable number.

When used. Daily portfolio risk reporting, limit setting and monitoring, capital allocation, and client/board risk communication.

Methodology. Three main approaches: historical simulation (revalue the portfolio over a window of historical return scenarios), parametric/variance-covariance (assume a distribution and use volatilities and correlations), and Monte Carlo simulation (generate correlated risk-factor paths). Each requires a clean risk-factor mapping, a return window, and decisions on decay weighting and scaling across horizons.

Inputs / outputs. Inputs: positions, risk-factor sensitivities, historical returns/volatilities/correlations, confidence level and horizon. Outputs: VaR figure, marginal/component VaR by position and factor, and backtesting exceptions.

Interview talking points

  • Explain why 99% 1-day VaR says nothing about the size of tail losses beyond the threshold — motivating expected shortfall.
  • Discuss historical vs Monte Carlo trade-offs: no distributional assumption vs. richer tail modelling and compute cost.
  • Describe VaR backtesting (Kupiec / traffic-light) and how exceptions trigger model review.

Skills: Statistics and distributions · Python / pandas / numpy · Risk-factor mapping

Expected Shortfall

ES / CVaRTail Risk Measurement
Open demo →

What it is. The average loss conditional on being in the tail beyond the VaR threshold — a coherent risk measure that captures the severity of extreme losses, not just their frequency.

When used. Tail-risk limits, regulatory market-risk frameworks (FRTB uses ES), and stress-aware portfolio sizing.

Methodology. Computed as the mean of losses exceeding the VaR quantile, typically from the same historical or Monte Carlo scenario set used for VaR. Being sub-additive, it rewards diversification consistently, addressing VaR's non-coherence.

Inputs / outputs. Inputs: full loss distribution / scenario P&L vector, confidence level. Outputs: ES value, contribution by desk/factor, and comparison to VaR to gauge tail heaviness.

Interview talking points

  • Articulate coherence (sub-additivity) and why regulators shifted toward ES post-crisis.
  • Show ES/VaR ratio as a quick read on tail fatness of a book.

Skills: Tail statistics · Scenario design · Coherent risk measures

Liquidity Stress Testing & Redemption Coverage

LSTLiquidity Risk
Open demo →

What it is. A framework that models a fund's ability to meet redemptions and margin calls under stress by comparing the time/cost to liquidate assets against projected outflows.

When used. Ongoing fund liquidity oversight, product design, redemption-gate/swing-pricing calibration, and regulatory liquidity reporting.

Methodology. Build a liquidity ladder bucketing assets by days-to-liquidate under normal and stressed haircuts (waterfall/slicing vs. pro-rata). Model liability side via historical and hypothetical redemption scenarios and margin-call paths, then compute coverage ratios and time-to-cash.

Inputs / outputs. Inputs: position-level average daily volume, bid-ask/haircuts, investor concentration, redemption history, margin terms. Outputs: liquidity buckets, redemption coverage ratio, days-to-liquidate, and recommended liquidity tools.

Interview talking points

  • Contrast asset liquidity vs. funding liquidity and why both must be modelled together.
  • Explain the liquidity mismatch problem for daily-dealing funds holding less-liquid assets.
  • Discuss anti-dilution tools (swing pricing, ADLs, gates) and first-mover advantage.

Skills: Liquidity modelling · Market microstructure · Scenario analysis

Scenario Analysis & Stress Testing

Enterprise / Market Risk
Open demo →

What it is. The revaluation of a portfolio under defined shocks — historical (2008, 2020 COVID, 2022 gilt/rates), hypothetical, and reverse stress tests that ask what scenario would breach the firm.

When used. Board risk reporting, risk appetite calibration, capital planning, and pre-trade what-if analysis for large allocations.

Methodology. Define coherent multi-factor shocks (rates, spreads, equities, FX, vol), apply to risk-factor sensitivities or full revaluation, and aggregate P&L impact. Reverse stress testing inverts the process to find breaking-point scenarios.

Inputs / outputs. Inputs: shock definitions, sensitivities/full revaluation engine, correlation assumptions. Outputs: scenario P&L, largest contributors, and limit-utilisation under stress.

Interview talking points

  • Explain why stress testing complements VaR by capturing regime shifts and correlation breakdowns.
  • Describe reverse stress testing and its value for tail governance.

Skills: Scenario design · Multi-asset valuation · Communication

Credit Support Annex & Collateral Management

CSACounterparty Credit Risk
Open demo →

What it is. The ISDA Master Agreement collateral annex that governs margining of bilateral OTC derivatives — thresholds, minimum transfer amounts, eligible collateral, haircuts and independent amounts.

When used. OTC derivative onboarding, daily margin calls, counterparty exposure management and UMR compliance.

Methodology. Exposure is measured as mark-to-market plus potential future exposure; the CSA determines how much of that must be collateralised. Risk monitors net exposure after collateral, disputes, and settlement/collateral timing gaps.

Inputs / outputs. Inputs: trade MtM, CSA terms (threshold, MTA, IA), eligible collateral and haircuts, netting sets. Outputs: net counterparty exposure, margin calls, collateral funding needs.

Interview talking points

  • Walk through how threshold and MTA leave residual uncollateralised exposure.
  • Explain wrong-way risk and how CSA terms and initial margin mitigate it.
  • Link CSA terms to funding-liquidity risk during volatility spikes.

Skills: ISDA / CSA documentation · Counterparty exposure modelling · Collateral and funding

Factor & Attribution Risk Models

Portfolio Risk Analytics
Open demo →

What it is. Multi-factor models (e.g. Barra/Axioma-style) that decompose portfolio risk and return into systematic factors (style, industry, country) and idiosyncratic risk.

When used. Ex-ante risk decomposition, active-risk (tracking error) budgeting, and performance/risk attribution vs. benchmark.

Methodology. Estimate factor exposures and a factor covariance matrix, combine with specific risk to produce total and active risk, then attribute risk to factors and positions for the front office and clients.

Inputs / outputs. Inputs: holdings, benchmark, factor exposures and covariances. Outputs: tracking error, factor risk contributions, active-share and concentration diagnostics.

Interview talking points

  • Distinguish absolute risk vs. active risk / tracking error and when each matters.
  • Explain unintended factor bets surfaced by decomposition.

Skills: Factor modelling · Linear algebra · Attribution

Middle office

The middle office: flow, responsibilities & skills

On the buy-side, the middle office is the control and information layer between the front office (portfolio managers, traders and dealing desks) and the back office (fund accounting, custody and transfer agency). It owns the integrity of positions, valuations, exposures and mandate compliance — turning raw trades into trustworthy risk, performance and client reporting. A strong buy-side middle office lets PMs take risk with confidence that limits are monitored, NAV is defensible, and every trade is captured, valued and reconciled independently of the people who executed it.

1

Trade support / Investment operations

Capture executions from the OMS/EMS (Aladdin, Charles River, SimCorp), enrich with SSIs and account coding, and affirm/confirm with brokers and counterparties on trade date.

→↓
2

Independent valuation & IPV

Valuation / Pricing oversight

Strike independent prices from vendor and consensus sources, run stale-price and tolerance checks, and apply fair-value adjustments and liquidity haircuts for hard-to-value names.

→↓
3

Position & cash reconciliation

Reconciliations / Middle office

Reconcile positions, cash and corporate actions against the custodian, prime broker and fund administrator; investigate and clear breaks before NAV strike.

→↓
4

Mandate & guideline monitoring

Investment compliance / MO

Run pre- and post-trade checks against IMA, UCITS/prospectus and regulatory limits; escalate active vs passive breaches with a documented remediation trail.

→↓
5

Risk & performance reporting

Investment risk / Performance

Produce VaR, exposure, liquidity and factor risk plus performance attribution, packaged for PMs, the risk committee and clients.

→↓
6

Collateral & margin

Collateral management

Agree daily VM/IM calls under CSAs, manage tri-party and cleared margin, resolve disputes and optimise eligible collateral.

→↓
7

NAV oversight & client reporting

MO / Client service

Review the administrator's NAV, sign off tolerance checks, and deliver factsheets, investor statements and regulatory returns.

Core responsibilities

  • Guarantee complete, accurate and timely trade capture across all asset classes and account structures.
  • Own independent price verification and the fair-value/pricing-exception process feeding NAV.
  • Reconcile positions, cash and corporate actions to custodians, prime brokers and administrators and clear breaks.
  • Monitor investment guidelines, mandate and regulatory limits, and manage the breach lifecycle end to end.
  • Produce and distribute independent risk, exposure, liquidity and performance reporting.
  • Manage collateral and margin workflows, disputes and eligible-collateral optimisation.
  • Oversee the administrator's NAV and act as control gatekeeper between front and back office.

Workflow

What a day looks like

Typical roles: Buy-Side Market Risk Analyst / Manager · Liquidity Risk Analyst · Counterparty / Credit Risk Analyst · Investment / Portfolio Risk Officer · Chief Risk Officer (CRO) at an asset manager or hedge fund

Morning

  • Run and validate the overnight risk batch — VaR, expected shortfall, sensitivities and limit utilisation across all funds and desks.
  • Review limit breaches and early-warning triggers; investigate P&L vs. risk (P&L attribution / clean vs. dirty P&L) for anomalies.
  • Check overnight market moves and news, and assess impact on largest exposures and crowded positions.
  • Confirm margin calls and collateral movements settled and reconcile counterparty exposure.

Midday

  • Provide pre-trade what-if analysis for portfolio managers sizing new allocations or hedges.
  • Meet with the front office to challenge concentrated or illiquid positions and discuss risk-budget usage.
  • Refresh liquidity ladders and redemption coverage, especially around subscription/redemption dealing dates.
  • Progress model validation, backtesting reviews, or methodology enhancements with quants.

Evening

  • Prepare daily and weekly risk packs for the risk committee, CRO and portfolio managers.
  • Update and sign off end-of-day limit monitoring and document any breaches and remediation.
  • Perform independent price verification / valuation checks on illiquid or hard-to-price positions.
  • Respond to client due-diligence questionnaires and regulatory data requests.

Crisis mode

  • Stand up intraday risk and liquidity monitoring; recompute exposures and coverage as markets move.
  • Model redemption and margin-call scenarios and advise on activating gates, swing pricing or asset sales.
  • Coordinate with treasury/operations on collateral, funding and counterparty concentration under stress.
  • Escalate to the CRO, risk committee and, where required, brief MAS on material issues.

Stakeholders: Portfolio managers and CIO (front office) · Chief Risk Officer and risk committee / board · Compliance and legal (ISDA, mandates, regulatory) · Treasury, operations and fund administrators · Prime brokers, custodians and OTC counterparties · Clients, consultants and MAS / other regulators

Fund Liquidity Stress Ladder & Redemption Coverage Simulator

“I modelled whether a fund can survive a redemption run — matching days-to-liquidate against stressed outflows and testing swing pricing and gates.”

An open-ended fund with daily dealing holds assets of varying liquidity. Risk must prove it can meet redemptions and margin calls under stress and recommend liquidity tools.

Approach

  1. Bucket assets into a liquidity ladder using ADV and stressed bid-ask/haircuts, under waterfall and pro-rata liquidation.

  3. Compute redemption coverage ratios, time-to-cash and residual liquidity after stress.

  5. Add reverse stress testing to find the redemption level that breaks the fund.

Reverse stress testing showed the fund breaks at a 28% single-day redemption — which directly informs the gate threshold.

OTC Counterparty Exposure & CSA Margin-Spiral Scenario

“I stress-tested bilateral OTC exposure through the CSA — showing how thresholds, haircuts and a vol spike turn counterparty risk into a funding-liquidity crunch.”

A fund trades OTC derivatives under ISDA/CSA terms with several dealers. Risk must quantify net exposure after collateral and the funding impact of a stressed margin spiral.

Approach

  1. Model a netting set of OTC trades with mark-to-market and a simple potential-future-exposure add-on.
  2. Apply CSA terms (threshold, MTA, initial amount, eligible collateral, haircuts) to derive net uncollateralised exposure.
  3. Shock market factors to trigger variation-margin calls and measure daily collateral outflows.
  4. Introduce wrong-way risk and a counterparty downgrade, and test collateral eligibility/haircut widening.
  5. Aggregate to a funding-liquidity view and compare against available liquidity buffers.

Stack: Python · numpy · ISDA/CSA logic · Scenario engine

Factor Risk Budgeting & Risk-Parity Allocator

“I built an allocator that budgets risk — not capital — across factors, then showed a PM that 70% of their 'diversified' risk came from a single equity-beta bet.”

A multi-asset book allocates by capital weight, but its true risk is concentrated in a few factors. Risk must decompose ex-ante risk by factor and rebalance to a target risk budget (equal-risk-contribution vs mean-variance).

Approach

  1. Build a factor model (equity beta, rates, credit, FX, value, momentum) and estimate a covariance matrix with shrinkage.
  2. Decompose portfolio volatility into marginal and percentage risk contributions per factor and per asset.
  3. Implement equal-risk-contribution (risk parity) and mean-variance optimisers with constraints (long-only, turnover, box limits).
  4. Compare capital weights vs risk weights and quantify the diversification ratio and effective number of bets.
  5. Backtest the risk-budgeted allocation vs the original and report realised risk contributions.

Stack: Python · numpy / scipy.optimize · pandas · cvxpy (optional) · Plotly

Ex-Ante Tracking Error & Active Risk Decomposition

“I decomposed a benchmark-relative fund's tracking error into the active bets actually driving it — and flagged an unintended sector tilt the PM didn't know they had.”

A benchmark-relative mandate must stay within an ex-ante tracking-error budget. Risk must measure active risk vs the benchmark, decompose it into sources, and monitor active share and unintended bets.

Approach

  1. Construct active weights (portfolio minus benchmark) and map to a common factor/risk model.
  2. Compute ex-ante tracking error and decompose into factor, sector, country and stock-specific contributions.
  3. Calculate active share and identify unintended vs intended active bets.
  4. Set a tracking-error limit with utilisation and alerts, and attribute day-over-day TE moves.
  5. Reconcile ex-ante TE against realised TE to validate the risk model.

Stack: Python · pandas / numpy · Factor/risk model · Plotly / dashboard

Talk track: Ex-ante TE was 3.1% against a 3.5% budget — fine on the surface. But the decomposition showed a third of it came from an unintended overweight to rate-sensitive sectors, a by-product of stock selection. Surfacing that let the PM neutralise the tilt and redeploy the risk budget into their actual convictions.

Multi-Asset Macro Scenario & Historical Stress Engine

“I built a scenario engine that reprices the whole book under 2008, COVID and a rate-shock scenario — and reverse-engineers the scenario that would breach the fund's loss limit.”

Boards and clients want to know 'what happens to us in the next crisis'. Risk must reprice a multi-asset portfolio under named historical and hypothetical macro scenarios and run reverse stress testing.

Approach

  1. Define a library of historical (GFC 2008, COVID 2020, 2022 rate shock) and hypothetical scenarios as risk-factor shocks.
  2. Propagate shocks through factor sensitivities and full/partial revaluation to portfolio P&L.
  3. Model second-round effects: liquidity haircuts, correlation breakdown and margin calls.
  4. Rank scenarios by impact and attribute losses to positions and factors.
  5. Add reverse stress testing to solve for the shock that hits a defined loss/limit threshold.

Stack: Python · pandas / numpy · Scenario config (YAML/JSON) · Plotly

Talk track: The COVID replay cost the book 14% with correlations converging to one, which broke the assumed diversification. Reverse stress testing then showed a simultaneous 150bp rates and 20% equity shock would hit the -10% board limit — a concrete, defensible number I could put in front of the risk committee.
